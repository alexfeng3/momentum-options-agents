We built this backtest to survive our own scrutiny before anyone else's. Parameters were fit only on 2011-2018, checked once on 2019-2021, and the 2022-2026 test window was run exactly once and never touched again to adjust a threshold, a weight, or a gate. Most hackathon backtests don't separate these periods at all; we treat that separation as the whole point.

The number we'd most want a judge to sit with is survivorship bias, because we quantified it against ourselves. Our headline results come from a 39-name universe that, honestly, was hand-picked in 2026 and contains the AI-era winners with the benefit of hindsight. So we reran the identical strategy on a second, mechanically screened universe of 951 names that we did not curate at all — it includes the delistings and bankruptcies the hand-picked list quietly omits. The strategy's alpha survives there, at roughly +28.7%/year, but about 40% of the hand-picked headline return turns out to be universe selection, not the strategy. We report that gap prominently rather than only showing the bigger number.

We also found and fixed a real bug the hard way. An earlier version of our backtester allocated all equity to stock and then sold option spreads collateralized by cash that was already spent — silent leverage that inflated our 15-year CAGR by about 8 percentage points and reversed our conclusion about whether the options overlay was even worth running. Nobody flagged this for us; we caught it in adversarial self-review. `portfolio.py` now enforces single-pot capital accounting with runtime assertions that raise rather than fail silently, pinned by dedicated tests so the bug structurally cannot come back.

We're equally direct about what this strategy is not: not market-neutral (beta 0.8), not low-drawdown (-47% in our worst validate-period stretch), not a large-cap strategy (the edge collapses to about 1%/year on calm large caps — it lives specifically in volatility), and the options overlay is priced with Black-Scholes rather than a real historical option chain, so treat that piece as an edge estimate, not achievable P&L. The earnings-drift sleeve, by contrast, runs on real SEC EDGAR filings, not simulated surprises, with two independent confirmation signals that both have to agree. All of this is backed by 64 automated tests, including ones that pin the capital invariants and prove the execution agent structurally cannot submit an order that risk didn't approve.
